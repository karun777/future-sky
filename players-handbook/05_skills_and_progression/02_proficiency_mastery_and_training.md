# Proficiency, Mastery, and Training

Skills in Future Sky do not improve automatically.

They improve because you use them, strain them, reflect on them, and deliberately train them.

This chapter explains how proficiency works, what mastery represents, and how characters actually get better over time.

---

## Proficiency Levels

Each skill has a level of proficiency.

Proficiency represents:
- Familiarity with the domain
- Reliability under pressure
- Ability to attempt increasingly complex actions

Proficiency is not binary.
It develops gradually.

---

## Broad Proficiency Bands

For clarity and pacing, proficiency is understood in broad bands rather than granular increments.

While exact values are system-specific, proficiency generally progresses through stages such as:
- **Untrained** — limited, risky, context-dependent
- **Familiar** — basic competence, frequent failure under pressure
- **Competent** — reliable in common situations
- **Advanced** — adaptable, confident, efficient
- **Mastered** — authoritative, shaping outcomes rather than reacting

These bands describe *capability*, not status.

---

## What Mastery Means

Mastery is not perfection.

Mastery represents:
- Reduced strain for aligned actions
- Greater control over outcomes
- Increased narrative authority in that domain
- Ability to teach or guide others
- Recognition by the world

Masters still fail.
Their failures are simply more consequential.

---

## Training Versus Use

Not all use leads to growth.

Skills improve through:
- Meaningful application under pressure
- Deliberate training during downtime
- Instruction or mentorship
- Long-term projects
- Transformative experience

Repeated trivial use yields diminishing returns.

---

## Deliberate Training

Deliberate training requires:
- Time
- Focus
- Appropriate conditions
- Sometimes tools or facilities
- Often guidance

Training is most effective when:
- You know what you are trying to improve
- You accept short-term inefficiency
- You allow room for failure

Training is work.

---

## Learning Through Failure

Failure is instructive.

Failing under pressure:
- Reveals limits
- Teaches caution
- Builds resilience
- Informs future strategy

Some of the most important growth occurs after things go wrong.

---

## Teaching and Mentorship

Characters can learn from others.

Mentorship:
- Accelerates learning
- Provides structure
- Reduces risk
- Transfers perspective, not just technique

A teacher shapes how a skill is understood — not just how it is performed.

---

## Cross-Training and Transfer

Skills are not isolated.

Experience in one domain may:
- Support learning in a related domain
- Reduce the cost of initial training
- Provide conceptual shortcuts

Cross-training encourages breadth without erasing specialisation.

---

## Limits to Growth

Growth has limits.

These limits may be imposed by:
- Physical or cognitive capacity
- Persistent injury or trauma
- Chronic strain
- Cultural or technological constraints
- Narrative choice

Limits create texture.
They give growth shape.

---

## Maintaining Skill

Skill degrades without use.

Neglect may lead to:
- Reduced reliability
- Increased strain
- Loss of edge

Maintenance matters — especially at higher levels of mastery.

---

## Why This Matters

This approach ensures that:
- Growth feels earned
- Training choices matter
- Failure contributes to development
- Mastery changes play meaningfully
- Optimisation does not eclipse narrative

Skills grow the way people do.

---

## A Final Orientation

Do not ask:
- “How fast can I level this?”

Ask instead:
- “What am I practising?”
- “Who am I learning from?”
- “What kind of practitioner am I becoming?”

Mastery is not rushed.

It is accumulated.
