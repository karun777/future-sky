# Tone, Pacing, and Pressure

Tone is the emotional texture of play.  
Pacing is how quickly things change.  
Pressure is what gives choices weight.

Together, they shape how *Future Sky* feels moment to moment.

This chapter explains how to use them deliberately.

---

## Tone Is Set Before Rules Are Used

Tone is established by:
- how you describe scenes
- what details you linger on
- how consequences are framed
- what you choose not to emphasise

Players will take cues from you immediately.

If the tone is unclear, mechanics will feel noisy or arbitrary.

---

## Naming the Tone

Early in a campaign, it helps to name the tone explicitly.

Examples:
- “Hopeful but brittle”
- “Quietly desperate”
- “Playful on the surface, serious underneath”
- “Mythic, but close to the ground”

You do not need consensus on wording — just alignment on feeling.

---

## Maintaining Tone Through Consequence

Tone is reinforced by what consequences *mean*.

For example:
- In a grounded tone, failure causes complications and scars.
- In a mythic tone, failure reshapes fate or reveals truth.
- In a tense tone, failure accelerates pressure.

Avoid tonal whiplash unless it is intentional.

---

## Pacing Is a Choice, Not a Reaction

Pacing is often mistaken for player speed.

In reality, pacing is controlled by:
- how often you introduce new information
- how long you let scenes breathe
- when you escalate or de-escalate pressure

You are allowed to slow down.
You are allowed to cut forward.

---

## Signs to Slow Down

Consider slowing pacing when:
- players hesitate repeatedly
- scenes feel emotionally dense
- multiple consequences are unresolved
- players begin talking out of character to process

Slowing down creates space for meaning.

---

## Signs to Speed Up

Consider increasing pacing when:
- players over-plan without acting
- scenes lose energy
- stakes feel abstract
- attention drifts

Speed creates urgency — but should not erase clarity.

---

## Pressure Is Not Constant

Pressure should rise and fall.

Continuous high pressure leads to fatigue.
Continuous low pressure leads to drift.

Use pressure in waves.

---

## Applying Pressure Gently

Pressure can be applied without urgency.

Examples:
- A resource slowly dwindles
- A rumour spreads
- A faction grows impatient
- A location becomes unstable

These pressures do not demand immediate action — but they accumulate.

---

## Applying Pressure Sharply

Sharp pressure compresses time and options.

Examples:
- A countdown begins
- An enemy advances
- A window closes
- A mistake becomes public

Sharp pressure should be signposted clearly.

---

## Pressure Without Punishment

Pressure should clarify choice, not punish indecision.

If players feel trapped rather than challenged:
- ease off
- reframe stakes
- offer partial paths forward

Pressure works when players feel *seen* by the system.

---

## Modulating Pressure Mid-Scene

You can adjust pressure dynamically by:
- introducing or removing time constraints
- shifting attention to consequences
- changing the environment
- adding or removing witnesses

Do this transparently when possible.

---

## Using Silence

Silence is a powerful pacing tool.

A pause after a consequence:
- lets it land
- invites reflection
- signals importance

Do not rush to fill silence unless players appear stuck.

---

## Ending Scenes Cleanly

A scene should end when:
- the question it posed has changed
- pressure has shifted
- new information reframes intent

End scenes deliberately.
Unclear endings create drift.

---

## A Final Orientation

When unsure how to proceed, ask yourself:

- What emotion should this moment leave behind?
- Is the pace serving clarity or avoidance?
- Is pressure revealing or obscuring choice?

Answering these will guide your next move.
