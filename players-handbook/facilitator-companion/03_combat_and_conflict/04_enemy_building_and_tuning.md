# Enemy Building and Tuning

Enemies are not stat blocks.

They are expressions of pressure, ideology, environment, and consequence.

This chapter shows you how to build and tune enemies so they challenge players without flattening creativity or dragging play into attrition.

---

## What Enemies Represent

An enemy represents one or more of the following:
- opposition to player goals
- embodiment of a faction or force
- consequence made manifest
- obstacle created by past choices

If an enemy exists, it exists *for a reason*.

---

## Start with Intent, Not Numbers

Before assigning mechanics, answer:
- What does this enemy want?
- What are they protecting?
- What would make them retreat, surrender, or escalate?

Intent shapes behaviour far more than stats.

---

## Threat Is Relative

Threat is not absolute.

An enemy’s danger depends on:
- context
- numbers
- terrain
- preparation
- player state

The same enemy can be trivial or terrifying in different situations.

---

## Enemy Roles

Most enemies fit one or more roles:
- **Pressure** – forces movement or urgency
- **Control** – restricts options
- **Damage** – threatens harm directly
- **Support** – enables others
- **Signal** – communicates danger or escalation

You do not need all roles in every encounter.

---

## Build for the Encounter Length You Want

Decide up front:
- Is this a brief clash?
- A sustained fight?
- A delaying action?
- A turning point?

Tune:
- durability
- damage
- morale

to support that length.

---

## Fewer Abilities, Clearer Play

Enemies work best when:
- abilities are legible
- behaviour is predictable
- danger is signposted

One or two strong abilities are better than five forgettable ones.

---

## Enemy Behaviour Matters More Than Math

An enemy who:
- fights intelligently
- uses terrain
- retreats when threatened
- targets objectives

will feel more dangerous than one with inflated numbers.

---

## Tuning Difficulty on the Fly

You are allowed to adjust during play.

You may tune by:
- changing enemy morale
- introducing reinforcements late or early
- shifting tactics
- altering environmental factors

Avoid sudden unexplained stat changes.
Let the world justify the shift.

---

## Signals Before Punishment

If an enemy is lethal, signal it:
- reputation
- visible gear
- environmental warning
- NPC reactions

Players should feel they *chose* to engage.

---

## Scaling for Groups

When scaling encounters:
- add enemies before inflating stats
- diversify roles rather than stacking damage
- let allies or terrain matter

Large groups increase complexity faster than danger.

---

## Bosses and Named Threats

Bosses should:
- change the battlefield
- have clear phases or shifts
- provoke player adaptation

A boss fight is about *learning*, not endurance.

---

## Non-Combat Enemies

Some enemies are:
- systems
- environments
- social forces
- time pressure

Treat these with the same respect as combat threats.

---

## When Enemies Retreat or Yield

Enemies should:
- value survival
- respond to losses
- remember encounters

A fleeing enemy can be more interesting than a dead one.

---

## Aftermath and Fallout

After an encounter, consider:
- who noticed
- what resources were lost
- what narratives were created

Enemies do not disappear when combat ends.

---

## Facilitator Prompt

When unsure how hard to push, ask:
> “What consequence best honours player choice right now?”

Tune toward that.

---

## Final Orientation

Enemies are tools — not puzzles to solve or obstacles to grind through.

If players remember *why* they fought more than *how much damage* they took, you built the enemy well.
